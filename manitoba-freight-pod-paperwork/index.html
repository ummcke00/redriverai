The POD You Can't Find Is an Invoice You Can't Send

The freight itself almost always gets delivered. The truck shows up, the freight moves, the customer gets their load — Manitoba carriers and warehouses are good at the physical work, and most have been doing it for decades. What happens after the handshake at the dock is where the money leaks. The bill of lading rode in the cab. The proof of delivery got signed and pocketed. The trip sheet came back with the fuel surcharge scribbled in the margin and one legibility short of a filing system. And somewhere between the door pocket and the accounts receivable report, a chain of handoffs has to turn all of that paper into an invoice — with every handoff a chance for a page to go missing, a charge to vanish, or a bill to sit unsent because the paperwork that backs it can't be found.

This page breaks down the three moments freight paperwork goes wrong, the arithmetic of the paper trail, and what it looks like when the trail builds itself — with a 15-minute test you can run this week before believing any of it.

Three moments freight paperwork goes wrong

1. Capture: the paper rides, the system doesn't

The trip starts organized. The dispatch sheet is printed, the BOL is filled out, the driver has a route sheet with the day's stops in order. Then the day happens. PODs get signed at a dock in Portage la Prairie and go back in the door pocket. A route sheet comes home with coffee rings and a correction in pencil. The extra stop the customer added by phone at 10 a.m. exists as a memory and a text message. Every document that proves work was done spends the day loose in a cab, and whatever happens to it there — lost, soaked, left in the truck overnight, eaten by the seat — happened with no backup and no record. The capture moment is where tomorrow's invoice gets decided, and it's the moment nobody supervises.

2. The match: BOL against POD against invoice

Back at the office, the real work begins: proving that what was ordered, what shipped, and what got signed for are the same thing. The bill of lading says one piece count, the POD says another, the rate confirmation says a third — and a human with a highlighter reconciles them by hand, shipment by shipment. Miss the match and the invoice goes out wrong, which costs more later. Hold the invoice until the match is clean, and the invoice sits unbilled — the freight was delivered days or weeks ago, and the money for it is waiting on a piece of paper in a pile. Per-customer rules pile on top: this account bills per stop, that one per town, another only against signed PODs with the piece count filled in. Every rule lives in somebody's head, and the somebody is doing the matching between other tasks.

3. The close: month-end, and the search when a customer calls

The customer calls in February about a delivery from the 14th of January: "Can you send us the POD?" Now the search starts — the filing crate, the truck the load rode on, the driver who may or may not still have it, the other office, the other company's files entirely if the group runs more than one entity. Every minute of that search is unpaid, and the outcome is binary: either the paper turns up and the dispute ends, or it doesn't and the line gets written off because there's no proof. Month-end runs the same ritual in bulk — reconciling trip docs across terminals, chasing the day's missing PODs, building the on-time and volume report by hand from whatever got collected. The close is where the paperwork finally bills its real cost: in unbilled freight, written-off disputes, and somebody's evening spent looking for a page.

The arithmetic — fill in your own numbers

No industry averages here — your numbers hit harder because they're yours. Take one week and count: the minutes spent finding paperwork that already exists somewhere (the missing POD, the route sheet in the other truck), the minutes spent matching BOL against POD against rate con, the minutes spent rekeying what matched into the invoicing system. Add them up, multiply by your shipments that week, then by 52:

(find + match + rekey) minutes per shipment × shipments per week × 52 = the hours freight paperwork costs, per year.

Then three adders most carriers never count. First, the held invoices: freight delivered but not billed because the paperwork isn't clean — that's your money floating at your customer's interest rate instead of yours. Second, the missed accessorials: the fuel surcharge, extra stop, or wait time that happened but never made the invoice because it lived on a trip sheet and nobody transferred it. Third, the written-off disputes: the short count or damaged claim you paid on because the POD that would have defended you couldn't be found. Fill in the blanks and sit with the total.

What an automated POD trail does

This is a small system, and it runs itself once built:

  • POD captured at the door. Phone photo plus signature at delivery, filed against the shipment automatically — before the truck leaves the yard, the proof exists somewhere nobody can lose it.
  • The BOL travels with the load and comes back matched. Created at dispatch, updated at each stop, matched against the POD by rule instead of by a person with a highlighter. Discrepancies surface as alerts, not as month-end surprises.
  • Invoicing keys off delivery confirmation. POD received → invoice data assembled, with the per-town, per-stop and per-account rules applied the same way every time. The rekey stops existing, and so do the charges that used to vanish during it.
  • Trip docs reconcile across terminals and companies. Same system, same shipment record, whether the paperwork came back through one terminal or three — the cross-entity version of "which file is it in" disappears.
  • Carrier onboarding packs assemble themselves. New partner carrier joins, the required document set generates and tracks its own signatures and expiry dates, instead of living as a folder of copies.
  • Owner alerts only where you add value: a POD still missing two hours after delivery, a piece count that doesn't match the BOL, a route that hasn't closed out. Silence means everything delivered, matched, and billed.
  • The KPI report writes itself. On-time percentage, deliveries per route, claims by lane — assembled from the same records the invoicing already used, not rebuilt by hand from the shoebox.

The 15-minute test (run it this week)

Before you believe any of the above, run this — it costs one coffee break:

  • Trace one POD from signature to invoice. Pick a delivery from last week and follow the paper: where did the POD physically go after the signature? How many hands touched it before it became an invoice line? Count the hops and the hours between them. Every hop is a chance to lose the page that proves you're owed money.
  • Open your AR aging and find the held invoices. Which outstanding amounts are waiting on paperwork rather than on the customer? Sum them. That number is cash you've already earned, floating free.
  • Search for one POD a customer asked about. Pull last month's "can you send the POD" emails, pick one, and time yourself finding the document. If the answer involves more than one of: a filing crate, a truck cab, or a phone call to a driver, you've measured the search tax.
  • Check one invoice's accessorial lines against its trip sheet. Fuel surcharge, extra stops, wait time. If the invoice matches the sheet exactly, you've solved this — genuinely rare, and worth knowing. If lines are missing, that was real money this month and every month.

Write the numbers down. That's your baseline, and it's the same sheet the free audit starts from.
