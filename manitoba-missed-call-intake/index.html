Every Missed Call Is a Job You Never Quoted

The most expensive call a shop takes is the one nobody answered. Not the angry customer, not the tire-kicker — the one that rang out Tuesday at 7:40 while you were under a truck, went to voicemail, and died there. A job you never quoted leaves no paperwork. It doesn't show up on any board, it never becomes a line item, and nobody argues about it at year-end because nobody knows it existed. It's just a hole where revenue was.

You already know this happens. What's worth doing is catching the three specific moments it happens — because they're not bad luck. They're built into the standard setup: two phones, one owner, and hands that are usually full.

Three moments the phone loses the job

1. After hours, the caller hangs up on your voicemail

Breaks don't keep business hours. The furnace dies at 8 p.m., the pipe lets go at 6:30, the truck's down and the load has to move by morning. The caller hears "we're not available right now, leave a message" and does fast math: leave a message, wait, maybe get a callback tomorrow, maybe. The next name on the list is one thumb-scroll away, and its phone is ringing live right now. Some people leave messages. Most don't. And the ones who don't are invisible — no number logged, no voicemail to return, nothing to fix the next morning. Even the messages that do get left sit in the box until somebody with a free hand opens it, which is usually tomorrow, which is usually too late for the caller who needed tonight.

2. Mid-job, the line is busy — or answered by half of you

The phone rings while you're on the machine, up the ladder, or elbow-deep in the exact thing the caller is phoning about. You can't answer, so it rings out. Or you answer with half your attention and give a half answer — "can I call you back?" — and the callback loses to the next emergency half the time. Answering mid-job costs focus on the job in your hands. Not answering costs the caller. You pay one or the other, every time it rings, and it rings a lot.

3. The counter handoff loses the details

Whoever answers usually isn't the one who quotes. They catch a name and a number on whatever's handy — the back of a receipt, the margin of a work order, a text to themselves. What gets written down: name, number, "wants an estimate." What got said: the model number, the measurements, the access problem, the deadline, the part about how this is the second time it's failed. By the time the quote happens, the caller has to give it all again — and being asked to repeat yourself reads exactly like starting over with somebody new. Some customers repeat themselves. The rest call whoever wrote it down the first time.

Sounds familiar? It should — we've been taking notes

We keep research notes on Manitoba operations — public information, not audits of anyone's shop — and the pattern shows up again and again. Our notes describe a 50-year Winnipeg roofing firm doing "estimates + 24/7 emergency dispatch" — a "phones-first operation." A Winnipeg custom metal fab shop whose "custom sheet-metal job tickets, measure sheets and residential/commercial install schedules are likely run on paper and phone calls." A custom cabinet maker whose "customer order specs and installation scheduling flow from showroom consultations to a colony factory, likely on paper and phone." None of that is a knock on these businesses — it's the standard setup, and the standard setup drops calls. Phones-first is the normal condition of the trade.

The arithmetic — fill in your own numbers

We're not going to quote you an industry average, because we don't have to — you have better numbers, and they hit harder because they're yours. Count last week's calls that rang out or died in voicemail: the voicemail log, the missed-call list, your memory of the interruptions while you were working. Then run the formula:

missed calls per week × your close rate × your average job = work that walked, per week. × 52 to see it as a year.

Two honest footnotes. First: if the first blank is genuinely zero, close the tab — you've solved this and you've earned the rest of your day. Second: the formula undersells the after-hours ones. A call at 8 p.m. that went to voicemail had better-than-average odds of being urgent, and urgency is the caller least willing to wait for a callback. Fill in the blanks and sit with the number for a second.

What an AI intake line does

This is a small system, and it runs itself once built:

  • It answers every call, every hour. First ring, 2 a.m. included. No hold music, no full voicemail box, no "leave a message" unless you want one.
  • It captures the details, not just the number. Name, callback number, what's needed, where, and when — the things the counter scrap usually loses.
  • It texts you a summary. Between jobs you glance at your phone: who called, what they need, how urgent. Nothing waits in a box to be discovered Thursday.
  • It books into the calendar. The standard asks — estimates, service windows, deliveries — get slotted without a round of phone tag.
  • It routes true emergencies. "The shop is flooding" wakes you. "Price on a fence post" waits until morning with the details saved. You set the line; the phone obeys it.

The 15-minute test (run it on your own phone)

Before you believe any of the above, run this — it costs one coffee break:

  • Call your own number after hours. Count the rings. Listen to your greeting the way a customer would. Would you leave a message — or keep scrolling?
  • Call mid-job. Have someone phone you while you're on the machine. Count the rings again, and notice what answering costs the task in your hands.
  • Leave a test message. Start a timer. How long does it sit before anyone calls back? That number, in hours, is what your after-hours callers are living with right now.

Write the three numbers down — rings, message survival, callback time. That's your baseline. If it's ugly, the formula above just grew a first blank you can fill in honestly.
