The Jobs You Can't See Are Hours You Can't Bill

The parts almost always get made. The laser runs, the weld gets laid, the press cycles on schedule — Manitoba job shops are good at the physical work, and most have been doing it for decades. What happens to the paper riding along with the job is where the money leaks. The job traveler leaves the office clean and comes back — if it comes back — with quantities in the margin, a setup change in marker, and a QC initial nobody can attribute. And somewhere between the router rack and the invoice, that paper is supposed to answer two questions: where is the job right now, and what did it actually cost to make. Most weeks, it can't answer either one.

This page breaks down the three moments shop-floor paper goes wrong, the arithmetic of the traveler trail, and what it looks like when the traveler updates itself — with a 15-minute test you can run this week before believing any of it.

Three moments the paper traveler goes wrong

1. At the machine: the traveler is a routing slip, not a record

The job starts organized. The traveler prints with the router, the setup sheet and the stage list clipped to the front. Then the shift happens. The operator notes the finished count in pencil between cycles, because the next setup is already waiting. The setup sheet gets revised at the machine — a feed change, a fixture swap — and the revision never makes it back to the office copy. The first-article inspection passes and the sheet goes in the binder by the laser table, because that's where the binder lives. Every record of what actually happened spends the day on a clipboard in the hands of the busiest person in the building, written in the ten seconds they can spare — and whatever made it onto that paper is the only version the company will ever have.

2. Mid-job: the status hunt

Ask the office where a job is and watch what it costs: somebody walks the floor, or shouts over a machine, or makes a phone call that goes to voicemail because the operator's hands are full. Five minutes here, ten there, multiplied by every customer who's anxious because they can't see status anywhere — and by every quote that goes out blind because quoting can't see which stage is jammed. The cruel part is the answer is almost always fine: "it's at welding, ships Thursday." But Thursday came from a spreadsheet somebody updated Tuesday, and the answer cost a person mid-job. The status existed the whole time. It was standing at a machine, in pencil, where nobody in the office could read it.

3. Close-out: the QC binder, and the actuals nobody has

The job ships, and the paperwork scatters in three directions. The traveler goes on the rack — or rides in the box with the parts. The QC sign-offs stay in the inspection binder. And the invoice gets written from the original quote, because that's the only set of numbers the office actually has. The hours the job really took, the material it really consumed, the setup that ran twice — none of it lands anywhere an estimator or an accountant will ever look. Three months later, when a customer disputes a dimension, the first-article sheet that would end the argument is a binder hunt. The close is where the paper traveler bills its real cost: in invoices that can't defend themselves, and in quotes that repeat last job's errors because nobody ever learned what last job actually cost.

Sounds familiar? It should — we've been taking notes

We keep research notes on Manitoba operations — public information, not audits of anyone's shop — and the paper traveler shows up in them constantly. Our notes on a multi-stage contract metal fabricator serving OEM customers read: "Job travelers through laser cutting, welding, forming, stamping and assembly stages are likely tracked on paper routers, with quoting and on-time-delivery status kept in spreadsheets." An injection molder with a formal quality-assurance program: "Production job tickets, material/color specs and quality assurance sign-offs per molding run are likely handled on paper forms and emails." A family-owned tool-and-die and custom molding shop: "Mold-making and injection-molding job orders plus QC inspection sheets for each press run are likely managed on paper and whiteboards." A structural steel fabricator and galvanizer: "ISO 9001 quality records, galvanizing batch traceability and job costing across two production lines are likely maintained on paper forms and spreadsheets." And a custom cabinet shop: "Contract-to-production kitchen cabinet jobs with measure sheets, 3D design approvals and install dates are likely coordinated on paper." None of that is a knock on these businesses. The paper traveler is the standard setup — and the standard setup bills the same tax every week.

The arithmetic — fill in your own numbers

No industry averages here — your numbers hit harder because they're yours. Take one week and count: the minutes spent answering status questions by walking the floor or working the phone tree (find), the minutes spent rekeying traveler scribbles into spreadsheets and invoices (update), and the minutes spent assembling actual hours and materials at close-out — usually spent discovering they don't exist (close). Add them up, multiply by your jobs that week, then by 52:

(find + update + close) minutes per job × jobs per week × 52 = the hours the paper traveler costs, per year.

Then three adders most shops never count. First, the late jobs discovered at shipping: the due date lived in Tuesday's spreadsheet, so the expedite, the overtime and the apology were never in the plan — they were in the moment somebody walked out to the truck. Second, the chased QC sign-offs: jobs physically finished but not closable, not invoiceable, until the inspection sheet turns up and gets signed — finished work sitting in receivables limbo. Third, and most expensive, quoted-versus-actual invisibility: without per-job actuals, every new quote repeats the last job's estimating error. Quote six hours, run nine, and the three hours don't just go unbilled — they go unlearned, and the next quote guesses six again. Fill in the blanks and sit with the total.

What an automated job traveler does

This is a small system, and it runs itself once built:

  • The traveler is captured and updated at the machine or terminal. A scan or a tap at operation start and finish, setup changes entered where they happen. The record is built by the person doing the work, in the ten seconds they're already standing there — the pencil stops being the only copy.
  • Job status is visible without walking the floor. Office, quoting and owner answer "where's the job?" from the same live record in ten seconds. Nobody's mid-job gets interrupted to produce an answer the system already knows, and promise dates come from the floor instead of from Tuesday.
  • QC sign-offs attach to the job. First-article inspections, per-run checks and sign-offs land on the job record with a name and a timestamp. The binder hunt becomes a link, an audit pulls the whole trail in minutes, and a disputed dimension has a defense that takes ten seconds to send.
  • Hours and materials attach to the job. Quoted versus actual becomes visible per job and per stage: the three-hour overrun shows up on Friday instead of never — and the next quote starts from what the last job actually took.
  • Late-job alerts to the owner only: a stage sitting past its due date, a traveler with no updates all shift, an inspection waiting on a signature. Silence means everything is running, signed and on schedule.
  • The KPI report writes itself. On-time delivery, cycle time by stage, quoted-versus-actual margin — assembled from the same records the floor already updates, not rebuilt by hand at month-end.

The 15-minute test (run it this week)

Before you believe any of the above, run this — it costs one coffee break:

  • Trace one job's traveler end to end. Pick a job that shipped last week and follow the paper: where is the traveler now, how many hands touched it, and how many of its pencilled numbers made it into any system? Count the hops and the hours between them. Every hop is a chance to lose the record of what the job actually cost.
  • Time one status hunt. The next "where's my order?" call, time yourself from ring to answer. If the path involves walking the floor or shouting over a machine, you've measured the interruption tax — now multiply it by last week's calls.
  • Count yesterday's shipped jobs with no actuals. How many of them have recorded actual hours and material against the quote? In a paper shop the honest answer is usually zero, and that's the number the quoting adder runs on.
  • Ask shipping how they last found out about a late job. Was it the schedule, or was it walking out to the truck and the parts weren't there? The answer prices the late-job adder.

Write the numbers down. That's your baseline, and it's the same sheet the free audit starts from.
