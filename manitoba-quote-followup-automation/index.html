The Quote You Never Followed Up Is the Most Expensive Paperwork You Own

Here's an arithmetic almost nobody in the trades runs: quotes sent last month versus jobs won from them. Most owners can answer the second half from memory. Almost none can answer the first — because the quotes live in an outbox, the follow-ups live in the owner's head, and the ones that went quiet live nowhere at all.

We ask about this in every audit we run, and the pattern is consistent: quotes go out promptly, then follow-up happens only when someone remembers. The customer who replied same-day gets the job. The customer who sat on it for two weeks — the one who was busy, not uninterested — never hears from anyone again, and signs with whichever competitor called back second.

Three quiet ways follow-up dies

1. Memory is the system

"I'll give Dave a call Thursday" survives until Thursday fills up with actual work. There's no list, so there's no lagging indicator — a quote that went quiet simply stops existing in anyone's attention. This isn't a discipline problem; it's what happens when the tracking system is a person with a phone and a full week.

2. One touch, then quit

The common pattern: quote emailed, one "just checking you got this" a few days later, silence, done — written off as a no. But people are not ignoring you; they're running their own week. Most jobs that eventually sign needed several touches over a couple of weeks, and each touch that adds something (a price option, a start-date opening, a photo from a similar job) reads as helpful rather than pushy. One touch and quit isn't persistence failing — it's persistence that was never scheduled to exist.

3. The follow-up lives nowhere

Even owners with real discipline are storing "call the roofing guy back" in a head that also stores payroll, parts orders, and which crew is where Friday. A follow-up that isn't attached to a date in something that speaks up is a hope, not a process. The test is simple: if the owner got the flu for a week, would every open quote get its touches? For most shops, no — and that's the whole finding.

What one recovered job a month is worth

Do this with your own numbers: whatever your average job bills, assume one follow-up a month rescues just one quote that would have gone quiet — not an aggressive assumption; the quiet ones are usually the majority. Multiply by twelve months. For most trades around the southeast that lands in the thousands-to-tens-of-thousands range per year, recovered from work you had already won the right to quote. No ad spend, no new leads, no new website — the leads already came in and asked for a price.

And the cost side is already paid: you or someone on your crew spent real hours building that quote. An unsent follow-up throws away the most expensive part of the sale — the part already completed.

What the automated version looks like

This is a small system, and it runs itself once built:

  • Every quote lands in one tracker automatically. Send the quote (or log it from your phone) and the record exists — customer, job, value, date. No spreadsheet discipline required; the logging is the side effect of quoting.
  • The follow-up ladder schedules itself. Day 3, day 7, day 14 — each touch short, useful, and easy to answer ("still weighing options? I can hold that price another two weeks"). Nothing goes out that you wouldn't say yourself.
  • A reply stops the ladder instantly. The moment the customer answers, the automated touches shut off — no customer ever gets chased after they've already responded, which is the embarrassment that keeps most people from automating this at all.
  • The calendar does the selling for you. Every touch carries a booking link, so "yes, let's talk" becomes a scheduled call without the four-email negotiation of finding a time.
  • You hear about it only when a human should decide. Owner alert when a reply needs judgment or a hot quote goes stale — silence otherwise. The system nags the customers, not you.

The 15-minute Monday-morning version (do it manually first)

Before any system: open your email outbox, pull every quote sent in the last 30 days, and list the ones that never got a reply past the first touch. Pick three and follow up today — personally, briefly, usefully. Then write the rest on a whiteboard with a date beside each. If that exercise books even one job, you've just proven the value of the automated version — which is the same ladder, running itself, every time, with reply detection so nobody ever gets pestered.

Same shape as everything else we automate

Capture at the source, a rule that runs without depending on memory, and alerts only where a human adds value. Our case study is the same pattern at bigger scale: 450 inspections a month that used to be retyped by hand now generate and email their own reports — about 37 hours a month back, with manual compilation at zero since the October go-live. Quote follow-up is that exact animal at a smaller size, and it's usually the fastest system we build because the data already exists in your outbox.
