The Hours You Spend Tracking Hours Are Unbilled

Every crew-based business in Manitoba runs the same Friday ritual. Somebody — usually the owner, because the task "isn't big enough" to hand off — collects the week's hours from wherever they live: the foreman's pocket notebook, the texts sent from the truck at quitting time, the chalk tally by the shop door, the sticky note that says "Kyle 44.5" with no explanation of the half. Then the retyping starts. Then the chasing: whose sheet is missing, who texted "40 lol," which job those Tuesday hours belonged to. Payroll gets finished at 9 p.m. instead of 4, and the whole exercise — collecting, retyping, chasing, correcting — was done on your time, off the clock, for free.

Tracking hours is the one admin task every crew business does weekly whether things are busy or slow, and it's done by hand almost everywhere. This page breaks down the three moments hand-captured hours go wrong, the arithmetic of the ritual, and what it looks like when the hours record themselves.

Three moments hand-captured hours go wrong

1. Capture: memory writes the round number

Hours get reported when the shift ends or the next morning — from memory, from the driver's seat, from the second beer. Memory bills in round numbers: 8, 10, 12, "the usual." The 6:47 becomes a 7 or a 6:30 depending on mood and who's asking, and the 40 minutes of Saturday loader time either makes the sheet or doesn't depending on whether anyone remembered to write it down before the weekend erased it. Nobody rounds down. The company pays for the rounding, every week, in a currency nobody audits because the original number doesn't exist anywhere but a memory.

2. The retype: where the extras vanish

Whatever the pocket produced has to be retyped into the payroll spreadsheet — and retyping is where money quietly disappears. The 15 minutes of emergency call-out on Wednesday. The second truck's driver who "just helped unload." The travel time between sites that one foreman logs and the other doesn't. Each retype is a transcription with a human filter, and the filter always points the same direction: toward whatever finishes fastest. Then it happens again, because the same hours usually get entered a second time into the job-cost sheet or the customer invoice — two or three places, three chances to disagree, and the disagreement surfaces months later as "why did this job lose money?"

3. The close: Friday night, and the cheque disputes after

Payroll night is where the ritual bills its real cost. The sheet that never came in → texts → waiting → "just put me down for the usual." The 4 p.m. finish becomes a 9 p.m. finish, every week, and it's your evening being spent on it. The quieter cost comes after: the employee who looks at their cheque and says "I worked the extra half, it's not on here" — and they're right, because the half lived in a pocket notebook. You either eat it or you spend twenty minutes becoming an auditor of your own crew's honesty, which is a worse way to spend a Friday than either of you deserves.

The arithmetic — fill in your own numbers

No industry averages here — you have better numbers, and they hit harder because they're yours. Count last week: the minutes spent collecting hours (texts, notes, walk-arounds), the minutes spent retyping them, the minutes spent Friday chasing what's missing. Add them up, multiply by 52:

(collect + retype + chase) minutes per week × 52 = the hours you spend tracking hours, per year. Price it at whatever your evening is worth.

Then two adders most shops never count. First, the rounding: half an hour of generous rounding per crew per day is real payroll money with no work attached. Second, the unbilled hours: on time-and-materials jobs, hours that got worked but never made the invoice because the ticket and the timesheet disagreed. One unbilled hour a week at your billable rate is its own line item. Fill in the blanks and sit with the total.

What automated time capture does

This is a small system, and it runs itself once built:

  • Clock in and out from the phone, at the job. No punch-clock hardware, no kiosk by the door. A button. If the crew can send a text, they can clock in.
  • Hours land in the payroll sheet with no retype. The spreadsheet fills itself — collect and retype stop existing as tasks.
  • Every hour attaches to its job automatically. The job-cost report and the invoice stop disagreeing with the timesheet, because they're the same data. The unbilled-hours leak closes.
  • The rules apply the same way every time. Overtime thresholds, break deductions, the rounding policy you chose once — applied by rule, not by whoever typed it that week.
  • Friday becomes approval, not assembly. You review a finished sheet, flag what looks wrong, approve. Nine p.m. becomes four o'clock.
  • Owner alerts only where you add value: a missing clock-out, the first overtime flag of the week, hours logged against a job that didn't schedule one. Silence means the sheet is clean.

The 15-minute test (run it this week)

Before you believe any of the above, run this — it costs one coffee break:

  • Trace one crew's Tuesday. How many physical places did their hours exist? Pocket → text → scrap paper → payroll spreadsheet → job-cost sheet. Count the hops. Every hop is a chance to be wrong that you're currently paying for.
  • Time one retype. Retype one day of one crew's hours into your payroll sheet with a stopwatch. Multiply by crews × days × 52. That's the annual retype tax, measured, not estimated.
  • Check one T&M job from last month. Hours on the invoice versus hours on the timesheet. If they match perfectly for every job, you've solved this — genuinely rare, and worth knowing.
  • Ask the crew when they actually report hours. If the honest answer is "when I get home" or "Friday morning, from memory," then capture-by-memory is your system of record — now you know what it's worth.

Write the numbers down. That's your baseline, and it's the same sheet the free audit starts from.
